# Argumentative essay — screen time and attention

> [!expected]
> Marking instructions that apply to the whole paper: spelling, grammar and
> presentation are taken into account; a script that answers the question
> asked is preferred to one that displays unrelated knowledge.

## E1 · Essay

```exercise
points: 20
time: 90
```

*"Constant access to screens has made sustained attention a skill we must
now deliberately train."*

Write an argumentative essay of roughly 700 words responding to this claim.
Take a considered position, examine at least one serious objection to it,
and support each argument with a precise example drawn from your reading,
your observation, or current affairs.

> [!hint]
> Before writing, list three reasons the claim might hold and two reasons
> it might not. Choose your plan only once you can see which side your
> evidence actually supports.

> [!expected]
> - The essay answers the **claim as stated** — the link between screen use
>   and *sustained attention* stays the thread. An essay about screens in
>   general is partly off-topic.
> - The position is **considered**: at least one serious counter-argument is
>   examined, not merely named and dismissed.
> - Each argument is **illustrated** by a precise, developed example.
> - The essay is **organised**: an introduction that frames the question,
>   deliberately ordered paragraphs with connectives, a conclusion that
>   answers.
> - Language is accurate and the register is appropriate.

> [!solution]
> One admissible shape among many — a concessive plan:
>
> **Introduction** — a concrete scene or a usage figure, restatement of the
> claim, announcement of the line of argument.
>
> **I. The claim holds in part**: fragmented attention as a measurable
> effect; the cost of task-switching; the rise of deliberate practices
> (deep work, reading routines) as evidence that training is now needed.
>
> **II. But the claim over-attributes**: attention was already a trained
> skill long before screens (monastic reading, study techniques); the
> variable may be *design for interruption* rather than screens as such;
> some screen practices sustain attention rather than erode it.
>
> **III. Resolution**: what deserves training is not attention in the
> abstract but the deliberate control of one's environment — which
> reframes the claim rather than accepting or rejecting it.
>
> A dialectical plan, or a clearly-taken side that honestly examines the
> opposing case, are equally admissible: the rubric marks the construction,
> not the opinion.

> [!rubric levels]
> | Profile | Floor |
> |---|---|
> | Blank, or does not address the claim at all | 1 |
> | No argumentation and no relevant example; the claim is merely restated | 2 |
> | Poorly handled: weak or absent plan, arguments asserted rather than supported, drifting off-topic | 6 |
> | A position, a plan and examples are present, BUT the treatment is partial or superficial, or the counter-argument is only named | 10 |
> | Clear position, coherent plan, arguments supported by developed examples, counter-argument genuinely examined | 13 |
> | Strong scripts: a controlled line of argument, well-chosen examples that do argumentative work, introduction and conclusion that frame and answer | 16 |
> | Outstanding scripts: the terms of the claim are themselves interrogated, the discussion is personal and rigorous — up to full marks (20) | 19 |
>
> Modulation: ±1 point for language quality. Floors are minimums, not
> ceilings: within a band, position by what the script achieves **above**
> the floor, not by its residual flaws. A script may earn full marks
> without being flawless if it is remarkable for the level expected.

> [!anchor score:12]
> Answers the claim and takes a side. Three arguments, each with an example,
> but the examples are asserted rather than analysed ("many studies show
> that…"). The counter-argument appears in one sentence and is dismissed
> without examination. Introduction restates the prompt; conclusion repeats
> the introduction. Language accurate throughout.

> [!anchor score:17]
> Takes a considered position and holds it. Examples are specific and are
> made to do work — one is analysed at length to show *why* it supports the
> argument. A serious objection is examined over a full paragraph before
> being answered. Clear progression with real transitions. Two clumsy
> sentences and one unsupported generalisation keep it below the top band.
